1 · Model

A known magnet: N42, 1.32 T, Ø6.35 × 12.70 mm

The model is derived in six steps. Each one is a standard result from magnetostatics, and the numbers in the equations update with the magnet on this page.

  1. A magnet is frozen magnetization

    NdFeB holds a nearly uniform magnetization M = Br/μ₀, which is 1.05 MA/m for Br = 1.32 T. In a diametric magnet it points across the 6.35 mm width, not along the length. Outside the magnet there is no electric current, so only the divergence of M creates field. It is equivalent to a "magnetic surface charge" σ = M·n̂ on the curved side, positive on the north half and negative on the south half. The flat ends carry none, because M is parallel to them.

  2. Far away it acts as one dipole

    Summing all the surface charge gives a net dipole moment equal to magnetization times volume.

    m = M · V = (B_r / μ₀) · π R² L = 0.42 A·m² (R = 3.175 mm, L = 12.70 mm)

  3. The dipole field has a fixed shape

    Directly below the magnet's midpoint, the direction to the sensor r̂ is perpendicular to the shaft. The dipole field is B = μ₀/(4πr³) · [3(m·r̂)r̂ − m]. Split into the part along r̂ (the chip's Z) and the part across it (the chip's Y), the factor 3 appears only in the radial part, so the radial part is twice the tangential part.

    B_r = μ₀ m cosθ / (2π r³) B_t = μ₀ m sinθ / (4π r³) B_x = 0

  4. Spinning the magnet draws an ellipse

    With the shaft angle θ = ωt, Br and Bt are sinusoids 90° apart. Together they trace an ellipse with semi-axes Br and Bt in the ratio 2 : 1 (κ = ½). The amplitude does not depend on speed, because at 54 Hz the field changes slowly enough to treat as static (Section 6, link 1). The ellipse sizes are therefore what the sensor measures.

  5. Finite size changes the numbers slightly

    Step 2 treats the magnet as a point. A real 12.7 mm-long magnet sampled at 12 mm is not small compared with the distance, so the page also integrates the surface charge over the actual cylinder (Eq. 2). The correction scales like (size/distance)². It is −19% at 12 mm, shrinking to about −2% at 37 mm (see the findings below).

  6. The sensor measures the field in digital counts

    The TLE493D uses Hall elements: a current through a thin plate gives a transverse voltage proportional to the field component normal to the plate. It has one set of elements per axis, a 12-bit conversion and a resolution of 0.13 mT per count (this is the ratio between your mT and LSB columns). At 37 mm the tangential amplitude is only about 5 counts, which matters for the discussion in Section 4.

4 · Why predictions and measurements differ

Reading the discrepancies

The radial field agrees within 5% at three of four distances, and the sideways field is off by 11–23% at three. This section takes each gap in turn and ranks the likely reasons, with a test for each. The table shows the change in distance that would remove each error (a negative shift means the sensor would have been closer), and the local slope n of the field law B ∝ r−n. It follows the settings in the side panel.

Explains 12 mm

Each run was placed by hand, to about ±0.5 mm

Because the field falls as r−3, a placement error of 1% in distance becomes a 3% error in field. At 12 mm, 0.5 mm is already a 12% change. The table shows that the radial errors correspond to shifts of about −0.7 mm at 12 mm and +0.1 to +0.4 mm at the others. Those are each within what a ruler gives, and each run was set up separately. At 12 mm the radial and tangential components point to nearly the same shift (about −0.7 and −0.6 mm), which is what a placement error produces. This is the simplest explanation, but it is a consistency argument, not a proof.

Test Repeat 12 mm three times, repositioning each time. If the spread is about ±10%, placement dominates.

Explains the far points

Magnet tolerances add up to about ±5%

The field is proportional to Br·D²·L. K&J quote ±0.1 mm on the dimensions, which is ±4% in volume for a 6.35 mm diameter. NdFeB remanence also varies by a few percent between batches, and the magnetization direction is only guaranteed to a few degrees. Together these can account for the +1.4% to +4.8% radial errors at 20, 30 and 37 mm.

Test Measure the diameter and length with calipers (you have already done the length) and use them in the left panel.

Explains the sideways field

The measured κ falls faster than the model

The model's tangential-to-radial ratio drops from to over 12 to 37 mm, but the measured ratio drops from to . A constant gain difference between the Y and Z channels would shift all four points by the same factor, so it cannot produce this trend on its own. The table shows the same thing: at 12 mm both components need the same distance shift, while at 30 and 37 mm the tangential component would need a shift several times larger than the radial one. The candidates are: (a) ferromagnetic parts next to the magnet, such as the motor's steel shaft, the gearbox and its screws, which extend and distort the transverse field; (b) the Y and Z Hall elements not sitting at exactly the same point inside the chip, which a 0.3 mm difference would change by 2–3% at 12 mm; (c) resolution: at 37 mm the tangential amplitude is only about 5 counts, so one count (0.13 mT) of systematic error is a 20% change.

Test Rotate the chip by 90° about its normal and repeat one distance. If the error follows the chip, it is the sensor. If it stays with the setup, it is the steel. Replacing the steel shaft with a non-magnetic one would also separate the two.

Ruled out by the data

Not the chip roll, axial offset, speed, temperature or noise

Chip roll about the radial axis would move tangential field into Bx. Bx is below 0.3 mT everywhere, which limits the roll to about 12° or less and the loss of tangential field to under 2.5%, much less than the 11–23% gaps. Axial offset of the sensor along the shaft would create a Bx of about 3.4 mT per millimetre at 12 mm (model), but the measured Bx is 0.13 mT, so the sensor was centred within about 0.05 mm. Speed and heating: the amplitude is flat to 0.2% across each record, the chip stayed within 21–23 °C, and the NdFeB temperature coefficient (about −0.1% per K) is negligible over that range. Noise: the ±0.03 mT scatter per revolution averages down over about 570 revolutions.

5 · Using the validated model

Field and torque at the impeller

With J fixed by calibration, the model gives the field anywhere in the midplane. This includes the impeller's magnets inside the tube. Torque on a magnet of moment mi is τ = mi × B. Because the driving field is an ellipse, the largest torque available swings between miBt and miBr each turn. The lower value is the conservative bound for staying synchronised.

The cube size and grade are placeholder examples (two 3 mm N42 cubes). Enter your own. The calculation assumes the cubes' moments add and sit in the magnet's midplane. It ignores fluid drag, friction and the cubes' own interaction.

6 · Physics of the magnetic pump

From motor to dye: six links of physics

In the simulator, the N20 motor spins the external magnet, the impeller inside the tube turns the opposite way, and dye moves from right to left. Each link in that chain is a physical law. Links 1 and 2 are checked directly by the sensor data. Links 3–6 combine the validated field with the impeller's magnets and geometry. Those use the example inputs below until you enter your real dimensions.

The impeller distance, cube size and cube grade come from section 5. Motor speed comes from the sample rate in section 7 divided by the measured samples per revolution. Fluid is water at 20 °C (ρ = 998 kg/m³, ν = 1.0 mm²/s).

Figure 6 | The driving field turns at an uneven rate. This is the rate at which the field direction at the sensor turns, relative to the magnet's rate, through one revolution. Dots come from the measured By and Bz at the distance chosen above Figure 2. The line is Eq. 5 with the model's κ. Below the magnet the field turns 1/κ ≈ 2 times faster than the magnet as the magnet passes sideways, and κ ≈ ½ as fast as it points at the sensor. That is the rhythm the impeller has to follow.

Experiments that would test links 3–6

  1. Flow against motor speed. Vary the motor voltage and time the dye front over a marked length. Link 5 predicts flow rising in proportion to speed until the impeller loses lock, then collapsing.
  2. Loss of lock against distance. Increase the gap and find where the impeller stops following. Link 3 predicts the critical load torque falls as r⁻³, using the field model tested in section 2.
  3. Reverse the motor. The impeller and the flow should both reverse. The flow rate need not be equal both ways, because the blades are not symmetric.
  4. Ramp the start. Starting at full speed may fail where a slow ramp locks. That is the pull-in limit of link 3.
7 · Methods

How the measured values were extracted

Figure 5 | Raw sensor stream, first 92 samples at 12 mm. The logger stores sample index only, with no timestamps. Each revolution spans about 18.3 samples.

  1. Each CSV (Infineon 3D-sensor GUI, master-controlled mode, 0.13 mT/LSB) was split into revolutions at the upward zero crossings of Bz, with linear interpolation between samples.
  2. Within each revolution, Bx, By and Bz were least-squares fitted to an offset plus first harmonic. The two transverse phasors give the field ellipse. Its semi-axes are Br (major) and Bt (minor), and its tilt is ψ. Semi-axes do not depend on how the chip is rolled about the shaft direction.
  3. Values are the mean ± SD over all complete revolutions: N = 566, 558, 597 and 566 at 12, 20, 30 and 37 mm. Chip temperature stayed between 21.2 and 23.1 °C.
  4. Shaft speed: period = samples per revolution. The logger GUI reports 1003 samples/s. Change it here if your recordings used a different rate: → enter a rate
